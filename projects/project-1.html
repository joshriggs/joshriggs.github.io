Overview

Start with context. What was the product? What problem were you solving? Who were the users? Give the reader enough background to understand why this project mattered. This is where you set the stage before getting into your process.

This section can be a few paragraphs. Don't be afraid to be specific — concrete details make case studies more believable and more interesting.

The Problem

What was broken, missing, or unclear? Use real data if you can — user research quotes, metrics, support ticket themes. The more specific you are about the problem, the more compelling your solution will seem.

Avoid vague statements like "users were confused." Instead: "In testing, 7 of 8 participants couldn't find the settings panel without help, and support tickets about account management were the #1 category for three consecutive quarters."

The Approach

Walk through your design process at a high level. What methods did you use? How did you frame the problem? What constraints shaped your decisions (time, engineering lift, existing patterns)?

This is also a good place to show range — if you ran workshops, did competitive analysis, built prototypes, tested with users, talk about it here. Show that you think before you design.

The Solution

This is the payoff. Show and describe the final design. Focus on the decisions that mattered most — not every detail, but the key moves that made the design work. Why did you choose this approach over alternatives?

Connect your decisions back to the problem. "We surfaced account settings in the top nav because research showed users looked there first — and we validated this in usability testing before shipping."

Outcome

What happened after you shipped? Quantify impact where you can — metrics, retention, NPS, revenue, support ticket reduction. Even qualitative outcomes ("this became a template for how our team now approaches onboarding") are worth noting.

End with a reflection if it feels right — what you'd do differently, what you learned, or what came next.
